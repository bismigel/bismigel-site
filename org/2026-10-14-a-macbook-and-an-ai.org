#+TITLE: A MacBook, an AI, and a cluster that stopped falling over
#+DATE: 2026-10-14
#+FILETAGS: homelab:learning:ha

#+begin_quote
*Building the lab* — a companion to [[file:2026-10-14-one-cluster-three-nodes.org][One cluster, three nodes]].
#+end_quote

I am not smart. I want to say that plainly, because a lot of homelab writing
assumes you already know the thing it's teaching. I don't. I learn by breaking
things, watching someone fix them, and asking questions I'm embarrassed to ask.

This is the story of how I got a thirteen-year-old MacBook into my cluster, and
how I let an AI do most of the actual work.

* The MacBook

I had two small machines running my lab. I wanted a third, because I'd read that
three is the smallest number where a cluster can lose a machine and keep going.
I didn't want to buy anything.

Under a pile of cables I had a 2012 13" MacBook Pro. It's the last one Apple made
that you can still open with a screwdriver — the drive and the memory come out.
I'd already put an SSD in it years ago. I plugged in an Ethernet cable (it has a
real network port, which is the whole point) and closed the lid.

Now it just needed an operating system that wasn't macOS.

* Letting the AI drive

Here is the honest part. I did not want to partition a disk by hand, install
NixOS, and then persuade a cluster to accept it. That's three things I'd mess up
in one night.

So I did it with an AI assistant instead. It's a helper that runs on my own
machine and can do things for me — a "clanker," I call it, affectionately. I gave
it the SSH key to the MacBook and a simple instruction: install NixOS on it,
matching how my other two boxes are set up, and make it a third node.

It asked me to explain, so I did. Then it went to work.

The whole thing happened in the terminal, and from my side it looked like a lot
of text going by, plus me occasionally typing "go" or "wait, why." It wiped the
Mac's disk. It installed NixOS over SSH, using a trick where it boots a tiny
system into memory first. It relabelled the machine, gave it a fixed address, and
told it how to join the other two.

I watched. I understood maybe a third of it.

* The parts that scared me

- *It erased the disk.* I said that was fine, and then held my breath anyway.
- *A join can fail quietly.* The new machine kept trying to join and kept timing
  out. The AI went looking, found that the firewall on the first machine wasn't
  letting the cluster's "talking" port through, opened it, and it joined. I would
  have stared at that forever.
- *It deleted a cluster member.* At one point it removed the Mac from the cluster
  on purpose so it could reinstall it cleanly. That's the kind of sentence that
  makes me nervous, but it explained why: a two-machine cluster is actually
  *worse* than one, because both have to be alive for anything to work.

* What I actually took away

None of this stuck the first time. But some of it did, eventually:

- *Two is worse than one.* A cluster stores its memory across machines, and it
  needs a majority to agree. With two, losing either one stops everything. With
  three, you can lose one. That's the whole reason to add a box at all.
- *The "backup" wasn't doing anything.* I trusted a nightly job to copy my most
  important keys somewhere safe. When the AI checked, it was failing every time —
  a file it needed was missing. I had a backup I believed in that didn't exist.
  That one kept me up a little.
- *"Local" is a real thing to a computer.* Some apps are happy with no password,
  but only if the request comes from the local network. The moment it comes from
  the internet, they demand a login. That's why the same app behaved differently
  depending on how I reached it, and I never would have guessed it.
- *I can ask dumb questions.* I asked a lot. "What is etcd." "Why did it break."
  "Is this going to delete my files." None of them were dumb.

* Where it ended up

The MacBook is a quiet little member of a three-machine cluster now. It does
almost nothing — it just helps the others agree — but it's the difference between
"if one machine dies everything stops" and "if one machine dies, shrug."

I don't fully understand it. I couldn't rebuild it from scratch by memory. But
I can *read* it now, and that's new.

#+begin_quote
If you're where I was — two machines, no confidence, afraid to type the wrong
thing — get a third one, and get a patient robot to walk you through it. And
before you trust a backup, watch it succeed at least once. Mine hadn't.
#+end_quote
