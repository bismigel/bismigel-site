Most of this lab is me learning in public. I write about the parts I barely understand,
because that is where the useful stories are. This post is different.

Observability is what I do for a living. Of everything in this lab, it is the one part I
am not faking. So when I say the monitoring here is better than it needs to be, take it
as a small brag. It is one.

* Most homelabs have a dashboard they open twice a year

The usual story goes like this. You install Grafana because it looks good in a
screenshot. You make one dashboard. Then you never look at it again. It is decoration.
Nothing on it is connected to anything that would tell *you* when something is wrong. So
you find out the lab broke the moment you try to use it.

That is the mistake I refuse to make. It is also the mistake I spend my working life
cleaning up after.

* If it is not in git, it does not exist

The rule for the monitoring is the same as the rule for everything else. It is written
as files in the repo. And it rebuilds itself from those files every time it starts.
Datasources, the dashboard, the alert rules, and where the alerts go — all of it. I have
never once logged into Grafana to *build* something. I only log in to look.

That sounds fussy. It is the whole trick. A dashboard you built by hand is one disk
failure away from gone. And it drifts the first time someone clicks the wrong thing. A
dashboard in git is a review away from correct. And it survives the machine it happens to
be running on. This lab is supposed to be nuke-proof. Monitoring is no exception.

* The stack, quickly

- *Prometheus* does the collecting. It reads the standard machine metrics from every
  host. And it reads the cluster's own numbers from inside Kubernetes — how many pods are
  running, how many storage volumes are healthy.
- *Grafana* is the window: one page, called Homelab Overview.
- *Alertmanager* is the part that reaches me. It decides which alerts deserve a message,
  and sends them to a Telegram bot. No email I will ignore, and no dashboard I have to
  remember to open.

* I only alert on what I would act on

Here is where my day job helps. The beginner move is to alert on everything. Then you get
buried, and you start ignoring alerts. That is worse than having none. So my list stays
short, boring, and short. Each alert is something I would actually get out of bed for:

- a machine has stopped answering;
- a thing I *meant* to watch has vanished, so I stop trusting the numbers I can still
  see;
- a disk is filling up, caught before it is full;
- memory has been pinned for a while — not a spike, a trend.

Four alerts. Every one means "do something". I would rather have four that I believe than
forty that I have muted.

* The brag: my backups tell on themselves

This is the part I am proudest of. And it comes straight out of the cluster post. There, I
found out my "backup" had been failing every night for who knows how long. I had a backup
I believed in that simply did not exist. The only reason I know that story is that I went
looking.

So now the backups report on themselves. Each backup is a scheduled job. If it fails, the
operating system itself sends an alert into the same pipeline as everything else. A dead
backup shows up in the same place, with the same shape, as a dead machine. I do not have
to remember to check. The lab tells me.

The first time I tested it, end to end — a deliberate failure, landing in Telegram a few
seconds later — I grinned like an idiot. That is the difference between a lab that merely
*has* monitoring and a lab I actually trust.

* One screen

Homelab Overview is deliberately a single screen. Across the top are the numbers I check
in a glance: machines answering, alerts firing, pods running, volumes healthy, restarts
in the last hour. Below that, the trends per machine: CPU, memory, disk, load. At the
bottom, a live table of anything currently firing.

That is all of it. No wall of graphs that nobody reads. If the top row is green and the
bottom table is empty, the lab is fine, and I can walk away.

* What I would tell you

If observability is not your thing, steal the shape of this, not the tools. Put your
monitoring config in git. Alert on few things. Send the alerts somewhere you will
actually see them. And watch your backups succeed at least once — or better, make them
scream when they do not.

I am not smart about most of this lab. But this part I know, and it shows.
