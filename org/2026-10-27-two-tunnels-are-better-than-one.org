I only started worrying about the front door after something else broke and I saw how
much depended on one machine. Everything public — the sites, the dashboards, the agent —
came in through one Cloudflare tunnel connector on one box. If that process died, or I
rolled it out badly, the whole lab went dark from the outside. I hadn't thought about it
until then, which is usually how I find these things.

* The shape of the problem

Everything public arrives through a Cloudflare Tunnel. A tunnel is made of *connectors*:
little processes that dial *out* to Cloudflare and get handed the traffic for it. I had
one, running as a NixOS service on the hosting box, and the tunnel's routing pointed at
that box's ingress:

#+begin_example
ciceley.cc        -> http://192.168.2.82:32080     # the hosting box's ingress
bismigel.dev      -> http://192.168.2.82:32080
...
#+end_example

One connector, on one machine. If it's down, nothing answers.

* The fix

Two parts, less clever than I expected:

1. Run a **second connector** on the media box — a second NixOS service off the same
   tunnel credentials (kept in SOPS).
2. Point every public hostname at the connector's **local** ingress
   (`http://127.0.0.1:32080`) instead of a hard-coded peer IP.

The second part is the one I didn't trust until I watched it work. A tunnel's ingress
rules name a service, and with `127.0.0.1` that service is the connector's *own* machine.
So each connector hands traffic to its own box, and Cloudflare picks whichever connector
is alive. One API call flips every hostname:

#+begin_src yaml
ingress:
  - hostname: ciceley.cc
    service: http://127.0.0.1:32080
  - hostname: bismigel.dev
    service: http://127.0.0.1:32080
  ...
  - service: http_status:404
#+end_src

* Where the ingress actually lives

Here's what I had wrong for a while. My first version stood up a whole second
ingress-nginx on the other box, because I assumed each connector needed its own. It
doesn't. There's one cluster, and ingress-nginx lives *in* it. The NodePort it listens on
(`32080`) is served by kube-proxy on every node, so `127.0.0.1:32080` on either box
reaches the same ingress. The second box only needed the second connector.

I tightened two things while I was in here. k3s now binds NodePorts to the **loopback**
address on every node, so `:32080` is reachable only from the box itself — the tunnel is
the only way in, and a LAN machine can't reach the ingress at all. That matters because
the ingress trusts an identity header Cloudflare adds; if the LAN could reach it
directly, that header could be faked. Now it can't.

* The honest caveat

This makes the *edge* highly available: the tunnel and the connectors survive losing one
process or one box, and the ingress is managed by the cluster rather than living on a
single machine. It does **not**, on its own, make everything behind it redundant — that's
the cluster's job, and a few things (the media stack) are pinned to one box on purpose.
The edge being up just means the door stays open; what's behind it is a separate story.

* What it buys

For a homelab this is the right place to stop. The failures I actually expect — a
crash-looping connector, a bad ingress rollout, a process dying — now mean "one box
serves instead of two" instead of "the whole edge is down". The tunnel shows two healthy
connections, the sites answer, and the front door stops being the thing I worry about.
For one extra NixOS service and a `curl`, that's a fair trade.
