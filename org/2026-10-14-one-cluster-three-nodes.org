For a while, my lab was two machines. Each one ran its own cluster of one machine. I
told myself it was safe, because each machine had its own Cloudflare connector. So if
one machine went down, the tunnel would stay up.

That was wrong. And it took me a long time to see why.

The connector is not the thing that serves my sites. My sites run on one machine. If
that machine dies, the second connector keeps the *tunnel* open. But it returns errors
for everything behind it. I had added safety to the part that rarely breaks. I had
added none to the part that matters. So I joined the two clusters into one.

* Why three

A cluster of one machine is just one machine. To survive losing a machine, you need
more than two. Why? The cluster keeps its memory in a place called etcd. To change
anything, most of the machines must agree. With two machines, you need both. So if one
dies, the cluster stops. Three is the smallest number that can lose one machine and
keep going.

I had two machines. The third turned out to be a 2012 MacBook Pro. It is the last
MacBook you can open with a screwdriver. I added a cheap SSD and an Ethernet cable. It
became a good third vote. It holds only etcd and the control plane. I marked it so
that nothing else lands on it.

* Letting the AI drive

Here is the honest part. I did not want to partition a disk by hand. I did not want to
install NixOS and then make a cluster accept it. That is three things to get wrong in
one night. So I asked an AI assistant to do it for me. I gave it the MacBook's SSH
key. My instruction was one line: install NixOS, match the other two machines, and
join the cluster. It erased the disk. It installed NixOS over SSH. It gave the machine
a fixed address. And it joined the cluster. I watched. I understood maybe a third of
it.

* What bit me

- *The firewall.* The join kept timing out. The port that etcd uses between machines
  was not open on the other machines. The new node could reach the API, but not its
  peers. I stared at pod logs for a long time before I thought to look at the machine
  firewall.
- *The mark.* Keeping the MacBook small was right for it. But it also means it cannot
  help *spread* apps for safety. A node that only runs the control plane keeps the
  cluster alive. It does not make the apps safe. That is a different job.
- *Storage.* I expected this one the least. My apps keep their data on the local disk
  of the machine they run on. So even with three healthy machines, a pod cannot just
  move. Its data is not there. Three machines gave me a strong control plane, not
  strong apps. Later, I added shared storage for the parts that need it.

* Making the control plane safe too

The cluster can survive losing a machine. But the thing that *manages* the cluster
should not be a single copy. So the GitOps controller now runs two copies of its web,
repo, and app-set parts. It also runs two copies of its controller (one active, one
waiting). One part is still a single copy: its cache. That is a weak point I have not
fixed.

* The lesson

Being safe is a property of a *service*, not of a box or a cable. A second tunnel
connector did not make my sites safe. It made the tunnel safe. Once I could say what I
wanted to survive — a whole machine going away — the design became much clearer. Three
machines that agree is stranger and harder than two machines that pretend. But it is
also the first version that is true.

The cluster was finally standing on three legs. With the hard part done, I could start
using it. And I could let a model take over the boring parts.
