I did a stupid thing, and I'm writing it down because the stupid thing had a
genuinely useful lesson hiding inside it, the way they usually do.

I tried to stream Jellyfin through my Cloudflare tunnel. Through the front door I
built specifically so that nothing would ever be directly exposed. Through *Zero
Trust*, with an authentication check on it and everything.

It buffered. Obviously it buffered. I just didn't know why for way too long.

* The grand tour of wrong suspects

The video stuttered, so I did what I always do and blamed the box.

** The server. Wrong.
Jellyfin transcodes on the fly, so I assumed the little i5 couldn't keep up. I
pulled up the transcode stats: it was encoding at about **280 frames per second**
— roughly ten times real time — and was a third of the way *ahead* of playback.
The server was bored. Not it.

** The disk. Also wrong.
The media pool is a mergerfs thing with NFS branches, and I'd had I/O trouble
before, so I checked. Reads came back at ~300 MB/s. Also bored.

** The throttle. Still wrong (but a fun detour).
I'd turned on transcoding throttling earlier as a power-saving trick. Surely it
was pausing and starving the client? I turned it off. It did not help.

** My internet. Deeply, gloriously wrong.
At this point the only suspect left was the connection, so I ran a speed test,
fully prepared to sigh.

#+begin_src shell
Ping     30.8 ms
Download 218 Mbit/s
Upload   450 Mbit/s
#+end_src

*Upload* was 450. Four hundred and fifty. My connection is a fire hose with a
garden tap confused about which direction is which. Ten megabits of video would
not trouble it. So it wasn't my internet either — and I had now ruled out every
single possibility except one.

* The actual problem, which was the path

Here's the clue that finally cracked it. I looked at where the player's requests
were *arriving from*, and they were coming from a **pod's IP address** inside the
cluster — the ingress — not from my laptop on the LAN. Which meant the stream
wasn't going laptop → server. It was going:

#+begin_example
laptop → my ISP → Cloudflare edge → back down the tunnel → ingress → Jellyfin
#+end_example

A same-room video, taking a round trip through the public internet and back,
because I'd opened the domain instead of the IP.

* And then the second, funnier problem

Fine, I thought — it's a detour, but it's a *fast* detour. Then I read the part of
Cloudflare's terms I'd skipped: the part where the free plan is explicitly not for
"serving video or a disproportionate percentage of ... large files." They don't
hand you a bandwidth meter and point at the number. They just don't want to be
your media server, and they're allowed to make that felt.

There is no knob that says "throttle this." There is no setting I missed. The
"throttle" *is* the policy. I went looking for a number to turn down and found a
paragraph instead.

And on top of that: every three-second chunk of video had to pass through
Cloudflare **Access** — a full authentication check, per fragment, for a movie. I
had built a bouncer for a cinema.

* The fix, which took one second

I opened Jellyfin at its LAN address instead — =http://192.168.2.81:8096= — and
it was instant. No tunnel, no edge, no auth-per-frame. Just a wire between two
machines that are sitting in the same room.

For watching *away* from home, the right answer is a VPN (I went with Tailscale),
so the video goes straight to the device and Cloudflare never sees a frame of it.
The tunnel is for the *door*: the dashboards, the APIs, the login pages. It is
not, and was never, a fire hose.

* The lesson

I keep relearning the same shape of thing. The tunnel is an extraordinary tool for
exactly what it's for — and I asked it to be something it isn't, then spent an
evening carefully measuring every part of the house except the one that was
broken.

"Use the right path for the job" sounds like advice so obvious it's useless. Except
I had a 450-megabit upload and a server idling at ten times real-time, and I still
chose the slow way — because the slow way had a nice lock on it, and convenience
wore a security badge, so I held the door open and waved it through.
