The site is public. The workstation config is public. The cluster repos are still
private, and I am planning to change that.

There is a difference between showing the output and showing the machine. A post is a
story I chose to tell. A repo is the hosts, the manifests, the decisions and the
mistakes — the whole working surface.

* What is actually exposed

The first time I pointed a real domain at a service in my house it felt wrong. Nothing
was listening on the router; the tunnel dials out. A tunnel removes an open port, not the
service. Anyone with the hostname reaches the login page, and the lock is authentication,
not the fact that the machine sits in my spare room.

The two connectors are from Part 7. I did not add the second because it was fun; one
machine had become a single point of failure for everything public.

"Public" gets used too loosely. Not everything is in the same bucket:

| Surface | Who can reach it | What stops them |
|---------+------------------+-----------------|
| Public apps: Jellyfin, Immich, Karakeep, Kavita | Anyone with the hostname | Each app's own login |
| Admin: ArgoCD, Grafana, Prometheus, Longhorn, media UIs, LazyLibrarian | Anyone who passes the identity check | Cloudflare Access |
| Hosts and cluster internals: SSH, the Kubernetes API, databases, exporters | Not published | No route from the tunnel |

Public does not mean anonymous. The media apps still ask for a password. The difference
is that Cloudflare Access is not in front of them, so the app login is the only gate.

Kavita is public because its mobile app cannot complete the Access login flow. That is a
trade-off I made on purpose, and it is the first gap I would close if I had to pick one.

* The fear became instrumentation

I did not become less nervous. I became nervous about specific things, and a specific
fear can become a check.

- The agent has read-only Kubernetes access and a Cloudflare proxy that only allows GET
  requests, so its curiosity cannot become a change. The write-capable Cloudflare token
  stays on the host, for the edge sync script.
- The edge is monitored from outside as well as inside. Tunnel status, connector count,
  zone state, certificate state and snapshot age all have alerts.
- A dead-man's switch runs on the workstation outside the cluster. Every other monitor
  lives inside the lab, so if the lab is down the monitor is down and silence looks like
  health. The outside timer is the exception.
- The backups fail loudly, and a weekly job restores the newest archive and checks the
  databases inside it. A backup I have not restored is a hope, not a measurement.
- Least privilege where it is easy: kube-state-metrics cannot read Secrets, and images
  are pinned by version or digest.
- Changes go through pull requests. The cluster self-heals, and an app that drifts for
  fifteen minutes sends an alert.

None of this makes the lab unhackable. It makes a failure smaller and louder, which is
the goal. The agent is its own story:
[[file:2026-10-16-deepseek-writes-the-boring-parts.org][AI in the lab]].

* Publishing the repo is another kind of exposure

A repo is not the working tree. It is the history, branches, tags, issues, pull requests,
Actions logs, artifacts, releases, and sometimes the wiki. When the switch flips, all of
it becomes public at once.

  I["Issues, PRs, Actions,<br/>releases, artifacts"] --> S
  S --> Q{"Secrets or personal data?"}
  Q -->|yes| F["Rotate, remove, redact"]
  F --> S
  Q -->|no| B["README, SECURITY, license<br/>branch protection, secret scanning"]
  B --> P["Public repo"]
#+end_src

The checklist I am working through. I am not publishing today.

1. **Inventory the surface.** List the files, then everything the files do not show:
   branches, tags, open pull requests, issues, Actions logs, artifacts, releases. The
   working tree is the smallest part of the job.
2. **Scan the tree and the history.** gitleaks or trufflehog for tokens, private keys,
   passwords and connection strings. Turn on GitHub secret scanning and push protection,
   which are free for public repositories. If something did land, rotate it first: a
   history rewrite does not reach forks, caches or screenshots.
3. **Remove personal and network details.** Names, email addresses, family usernames, LAN
   addresses, account IDs, zone IDs, tunnel IDs, hostnames, screenshots and logs all
   identify a person or a network. The technical reference keeps roles and paths, not
   addresses. I want a map, not a key.
4. **Check the security posture.** Tunnel-only access, Access on admin surfaces, app
   login on public apps, least-privilege RBAC, pinned images, weekly image scans,
   pull-request-only changes. A public repo should describe those controls accurately,
   not claim more than they do.
5. **Add repo hygiene.** A README that says what the repo is and is not, a license, a
   SECURITY.md with a contact path, and a plain statement that these manifests are
   specific to this hardware, network and Cloudflare account. It is not a turnkey
   product.
6. **Turn on the controls private repos could not have.** Public repos get branch
   protection and secret scanning on the free plan. The pre-push hook on the workstation
   was a workaround for private repos; public removes the excuse.
7. **Decide what stays private.** Operator notes, credential archives and anything that
   describes a live weakness. The map can be public. The keys cannot.

* Is it enough?

No. The gap list is still longer than I would like:

- No full disaster-recovery rehearsal. I have restored backups and checked them, but I
  have not rebuilt a host from nothing and timed it.
- No penetration test. I have tested the lab myself, so I know its weak spots.
- Kavita is public and protected by a password only. That is the weakest login surface.
- The free Cloudflare plan has no custom rate limiting. Access and app logins do most of
  the work.
- No image signing or SBOM yet.
- The private cluster repos cannot enforce branch protection on the free plan. That is
  one reason the public move matters: it makes the controls real instead of approximate.
- The public-repo audit is not finished. I am still walking the checklist.

The lab is in a better state than when it started. Two boxes became three, one connector
became two, one dashboard became five, a backup I hoped in became a backup I test, secrets
moved out of git, and hand edits became pull requests. That is not the same as finished.
Enough is a direction, not a finish line.

* What I would tell you

If you are about to expose a service, start with what is actually reachable, not with what
you are afraid of. Then make the failure small and loud. Then show your work.

I am still nervous about the internet seeing the lab, and about publishing the machine
behind it. The difference is that the nervousness has a list now, and the list is finite.

The detailed map is already public in the [[https://bismigel.dev/projects/bakuran-homelab/][technical reference]]. The repos are the next
step. When they go public, I will link them there.
